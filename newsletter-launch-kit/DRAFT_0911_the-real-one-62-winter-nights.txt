The marigolds are not decoration. They are directions.

Cempasuchil. The Nahuatl name means 20 flowers. The petals get torn off and scattered in a path from the grave to the door of the house, because the color and the smell are strong enough for the dead to follow.

We kept flowers on graves. We dropped the part where the flowers had a job.

Everything that looks decorative was load bearing once.

The sugar skull exists because a ban failed.

Spanish colonists arrived with alfenique, a sugar paste technique, and with the intention of stamping out the death rituals they found. Sugar was cheap and everywhere in New Spain. The skull had been central in Mesoamerica for centuries already.

So the skull came back, built out of the colonizers' own material. It reads as a fusion of cultures. It started as a way around a prohibition.

People do not give up their dead. They find another medium.

The oldest farming system in the Americas is 3 plants that take care of each other.

The Three Sisters, named by the Haudenosaunee. Corn gives the beans a pole to climb. The beans host bacteria on their roots that pull nitrogen straight out of the air and feed it back to the corn. Squash leaves shade the soil, hold the moisture in and choke out the weeds.

Western agriculture did not have a name for nitrogen fixation until the late 1800s. The system had been running on it for centuries by then.

Not having the word for something does not stop you from using it correctly.

The horn of plenty started as a child apologizing for something he broke.

Greek myth. Zeus was hidden on Crete as an infant and raised by Amalthea. He broke off her horn. To make up for it he blessed the horn so that whoever held it would find anything they wanted inside, endlessly. References to the horn of Amalthea survive from the 6th century BC.

The most recognizable symbol of abundance on an American table is a repair job.

A lot of plenty is just somebody making good on damage.

Going to church on November 5 was legally required in England for 253 years.

Guy Fawkes was found under Parliament with the gunpowder around midnight on November 4, 1605. By January 1606 James I had passed the Observance of 5th November Act, which made attendance at a thanksgiving service compulsory. It stayed on the books until 1859.

Bonfire Night is not a folk custom that grew on its own. It is a state holiday that outlived the law that created it.

Mandate a celebration long enough and it starts to feel like it was always yours.

The Native American moon names in your weather app mostly came out of a 1930s almanac.

The Maine Farmers' Almanac started printing lists of Indian full moon names in the 1930s. A few trace to Algonquin sources. Many are Old English, and some look like inventions by the almanacs themselves. The oldest citation for Hunter's Moon in the Oxford English Dictionary is from 1710, in English.

The names are beautiful. The credit is mostly wrong. Real nations kept real calendars, and those are worth learning instead.

Getting the credit right is part of giving it.

The turkey is not from Turkey and never has been.

Europeans were already eating helmeted guinea fowl out of Africa, shipped in through Ottoman trade routes, and they called those birds turkeys after the route. When they met the American bird in the 1500s it looked close enough that the name transferred. The guinea fowl eventually got its own name back.

The bird has been named after a shipping lane and a case of mistaken identity for 500 years.

Half of what we call things is just a receipt for who sold them to us.

NIGHT 9 of 62 - 2026-11-09 (Mon)

We know a good cranberry bounces because a farmer with a wooden leg got tired of the stairs.

John Peg Leg Webb, New Jersey, 1880. Instead of carrying his crop down the steps he poured it down. The firm berries rolled all the way to the bottom. The bruised ones stopped partway. The bounce board came out of that, and Lothrop Hayden of Carver, Massachusetts patented an improved separator in 1900.

Long before any of it, the Wampanoag were calling them sasumuneash and pounding them with fat and dried meat into something that kept for years.

Somebody was always there first, and usually doing more with it.

November 11 used to be the last big meal before 40 days of going without.

Martinmas. It closed the harvest and opened winter, and the centerpiece was a roast goose washed down with Saint Martin's wine, the first wine pressed from that year's grapes. The next morning began the Quadragesima Sancti Martini, a 40 day fast running to Christmas.

The feast was not the season. The feast was the door you went through on the way into the lean part.

This was Armistice Day for 35 years, and the name changed because the veterans asked.

The guns stopped on November 11, 1918. Wilson marked the first Armistice Day a year later, for the men who served in a single war. After the Second World War and Korea there were millions of veterans the day did not name, and their service organizations pressed Congress to widen it. Eisenhower signed the change on June 1, 1954.

The date never moved. Only who it was allowed to include.

Sometimes the honest fix is not a new day. It is a bigger one.

Nobody mentioned Plymouth Rock for 121 years.

The land was not empty when the Mayflower arrived. It had been emptied.

An epidemic tore through Wampanoag villages from 1616 to 1619, in the years right before the crossing. Whole communities were gone. Patuxet, the village the English settled on and renamed Plymouth, was standing there cleared, with fields already broken for planting.

The story is usually told as wilderness and survival. They moved into a place that had been a town, and the reason it was available is the part that gets left out.

An empty house is not the same as a house nobody wanted.

NIGHT 15 of 62 - 2026-11-15 (Sun)

The man in the Thanksgiving story spoke English because he had been kidnapped and sold.

Tisquantum was one of about 20 men taken off the coast by the English captain Thomas Hunt in 1614 and trafficked to Malaga to be sold. Franciscan friars got hold of the captives. He made it to England, learned the language, and worked his way back across the Atlantic as an interpreter.

He came home to Patuxet and found it empty. Everyone he had been taken from was dead. Then he spent his remaining years translating for the people living on the site.

Behind every convenient character in a founding story is somebody who had no good options.

Nobody at that meal thought they were starting a holiday.

A day of thanksgiving in the 1600s was an ordinary thing a governor proclaimed, for a good harvest, for rain, for a ship arriving, for a battle. There were many of them, for many reasons. The 1621 gathering was not called one, and no one recorded it as significant.

You will see it claimed online that Thanksgiving was created to mark a massacre. That specific causal link is not supported, and Snopes has taken it apart. Winthrop did proclaim a thanksgiving in 1637 after Mystic. It was one proclamation among many and it is not where this holiday comes from. The massacre is real and deserves its own telling, not a shortcut.

An editor wrote letters about this for 30 years before anyone in office answered her.

Sarah Josepha Hale ran Godey's Lady's Book, the most widely read magazine in America. From the 1830s on she petitioned governors and presidents to make a national thanksgiving day. In her letter to Lincoln on September 28, 1863 she noted she had been at it for 15 years as an editor. Several presidents had simply ignored her.

She was 74 when she wrote that one. Lincoln answered within a week.

Persistence is not a personality trait. It is a strategy with a long payback period.

NIGHT 21 of 62 - 2026-11-21 (Sat)
================================================================================
For 2 years America could not agree on what day Thanksgiving was.

In 1939 Roosevelt moved it a week earlier to stretch the Christmas shopping season. 32 states went along. 16 refused and kept the last Thursday. Texas, Colorado and Mississippi gave up and observed both. People called it Franksgiving.

Congress ended the argument in 1941 by fixing it to the fourth Thursday. Roosevelt signed that on December 26, 1941, 19 days after Pearl Harbor.

The date you think is ancient was settled by a shopping dispute inside living memory.

Per head of population, the deadliest war ever fought on this land started 54 years after that meal.

King Philip's War, 1675 to 1676. It was led by Metacom, Massasoit's own son. Measured against a population of roughly 52,000, the colonial death rate came to nearly twice the Civil War's. Native losses ran past 5,000, and at least 1,000 people were sold into slavery.

Metacom was shot in August 1676. His head was put on a pole at Plymouth and left there for 25 years. His wife and his 9 year old son were sold into slavery in Bermuda.

The alliance and the war have the same 2 families in them, one generation apart.

In 1970 Plymouth invited a Wampanoag man to speak at the 350th anniversary, then read his speech and took the invitation back.

Wamsutta Frank James, Aquinnah Wampanoag. The organizers asked to see it in advance. What he had written was history rather than pageant, so they withdrew the invitation.

A speech nobody was allowed to hear has now outlasted the banquet it was written for.

Giving thanks at harvest was already here. There were 13 of them a year.

The Wampanoag kept Thirteen Moons on the lunar year, and each moon carried its own thanksgiving. The first strawberries in June, with a ceremony about forgiveness. The first green beans. The tapping of the maples. The stretch of deep winter set aside for storytelling. There was also Nikkomosachmiawene, the Grand Sachem's Council Feast.

Thousands of years before anyone landed. The practice America adopted was already running here, 13 times a year, and it got attached to the wrong afternoon.

The part of this holiday worth keeping is the oldest part, and it was never ours to invent.

Black Friday has nothing to do with going into the black, and retailers know it.

Philadelphia police coined it in the 1950s and 60s. The Army Navy game was played there the Saturday after Thanksgiving, out of town fans came in early, the holiday shoppers were already out, and the result was gridlock the officers had to work. They called it Black Friday, and Black Saturday after it.

The accounting story, red ink to black ink, came later. It is the version retail prefers, because the real one is a complaint about them.

When a business explains where its own name came from, check who the story flatters.

NIGHT 29 of 62 - 2026-11-29 (Sun)
================================================================================
The first advent calendars were chalk marks on a door.

German Lutheran families in the mid 1800s counted the days to Christmas by chalking a line on a door or a cupboard each morning from December 1. Some parents did it with Bible verses, some with a small snack. In 1908 a Munich publisher named Gerhard Lang printed the first commercial one, copying the cardboard doors his own mother had made him as a boy.

It went from a chalk line to a printed sheet to a cardboard door to a chocolate to a bottle of gin in about a century.

Anticipation is the oldest part. Everything since is packaging.

Then first footing: whoever crosses your threshold first after midnight sets the tone for your year. Tradition wants a tall dark haired man, and he should arrive carrying a coin, bread, salt, coal and whisky. Money, food, flavour, warmth, good cheer.

The preference for dark hair is said to go back to the Viking invasions, when a fair haired stranger at your door after dark was not good news.

A superstition is often a very old piece of practical advice with the reason worn off.

England did not agree that today was New Year's Day until 1752.

For about 600 years, from 1155 to 1752, the English year began on March 25, Lady Day, one of the quarter days when rents and debts came due. Scotland had moved to January 1 a century and a half earlier. When England finally adopted the Gregorian calendar it also had to drop 11 days: Wednesday September 2, 1752 was followed by Thursday September 14.

The British tax year still starts on April 6 rather than April 1, because of those 11 missing days. A calendar correction from 1752 is still sitting in the paperwork.

WHAT GOT CUT, AND WHY
================================================================================

2 things I wanted to use and could not.

1. "Jingle Bells was written for Thanksgiving."
   CUT. Snopes finds no demonstrable evidence for it and traces the claim to syndicated
   newspaper quizzes in the 1980s. What IS solid: it was published in September 1857 as
   The One Horse Open Sleigh and the lyrics never mention Christmas, December or any
   holiday. That version is on the bench below and is usable. The Thanksgiving claim is not.

2. "Thanksgiving was created to celebrate the Pequot massacre."
   CUT as a causal claim, and this was already flagged in the campaign document. Winthrop
   did proclaim a day of thanksgiving in 1637 after Mystic. Days of thanksgiving were
   routine proclamations for many occasions and this is not the origin of the holiday.
   Night 18 handles it by naming the claim and taking it apart, which is what a series
   about wrong details has to do with a convenient wrong detail.

Also corrected while drafting, against my own earlier notes:
   The cranberry bounce is John Webb, 1880. The Hayden separator patent is 1900, not 1880.
   Sarah Josepha Hale's own letter says 15 years as an editor. I had 36 years from memory,
   which is not in the source, so the caption uses "from the 1830s on" and her own number.
   Thomas Nast's first Santa is January 3 1863. The definitive red suited one is 1881.
   Lead tinsel was never actually banned. The FDA lacked the evidence, and importers
   agreed voluntarily to stop after January 1 1972.

THE BENCH: 6 VERIFIED NIGHTS NOT USED
================================================================================

All checked to the same standard. Use any of these to swap out a night Amanda does not like,
or to cover an unexpected gap, without another research pass.

1. TINSEL. Invented in Nuremberg around 1610 out of extruded real silver, which tarnished.
   Lead replaced it because lead keeps its shine. Everyone says lead tinsel was banned. It
   was not. The FDA concluded in August 1971 that it was an unacceptable danger to infants
   but lacked evidence to declare it a health threat, so producers and importers agreed to
   stop after January 1 1972. Voluntary, not banned.
   SOURCE: Mental Floss on the history of tinsel; Wikipedia on tinsel and the 1971 FDA action.

2. THE FIRST CHRISTMAS CARD, 1843. Henry Cole had John Callcott Horsley design it, 1,000
   were printed, and Cole sold the ones he did not need for a shilling each under the
   pseudonym Felix Summerly. Critics attacked it for promoting drunkenness, because the
   family in the middle panel is raising a glass. Same year as A Christmas Carol.
   SOURCE: Victoria and Albert Museum on the first Christmas card; Postal Museum.

3. THE POINSETTIA. Called cuetlaxochitl in Nahuatl and grown in Mexico for centuries before
   Franciscans renamed it flor de Nochebuena. Joel Roberts Poinsett, the first US minister to
   an independent Mexico, came across it in Taxco in 1828 and took it home to South Carolina.
   The Ecke family of Encinitas, California later held up to 90 percent of the US wholesale
   market on a grafting technique they kept to themselves.
   SOURCE: Library of Congress on Poinsett and the poinsettia; The Hustle on the Ecke Ranch.

4. JINGLE BELLS. Published September 1857 as The One Horse Open Sleigh by James Lord
   Pierpont, retitled 2 years later. The lyrics do not mention Christmas, December or any
   holiday at all. The most recognizable Christmas song in the English language is a song
   about racing a sleigh. Do not use the Thanksgiving claim. See WHAT GOT CUT.
   SOURCE: Wikipedia on Jingle Bells; Snopes on the Thanksgiving attribution.

5. WASSAILING. From Old English waes hael, be well, answered with drink hael. The orchard
   version is the real one: pick the oldest or best producing apple tree, hang cider soaked
   toast in the branches for the tree spirits, then bang pots, fire shotguns and shout to
   drive off anything that would hurt next year's crop. First recorded at Fordwich, Kent in
   1585. Held at Twelfth Night, January 5, or Old Twelvey Night, January 17.
   SOURCE: Weald and Downland Living Museum on the history of wassailing; National Geographic.

6. CHRISTMAS BECOMES A US FEDERAL HOLIDAY, 1870. Grant signed it in June 1870 as a unity
   measure after the Civil War, and at first it applied only to federal workers in
   Washington DC. Alabama had made Christmas a legal state holiday back in 1836 and was
   largely ignored. Louisiana and Arkansas followed in 1838.
   SOURCE: TIME on Christmas as a national holiday; The Reconstruction Era on Grant, 1870.
