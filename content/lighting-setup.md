# Lighting the talking-to-camera videos

Written 09/08/2026. The problem: the house has bad light, so the footage that
converts best (Amanda's face, person to person) is the footage that looks
worst. Free fixes first, in the order they matter. Nothing here needs a
purchase.

## Free, ranked by how much it changes the shot

**1. Turn the overhead lights OFF.**
Ceiling lights are the single biggest reason home video looks like home video.
Light from straight above drops shadow into the eye sockets, under the nose,
and under the chin. It is the least flattering angle that exists and every
kitchen has it. Off. Always. Even in daylight.

**2. Face the window. Camera between you and the window.**
Window at your face, phone with its back to the window. Never the reverse. A
window behind you turns you into a silhouette and no amount of editing gets
that back.

**3. Sit close to the window. Within about 3 feet.**
The closer the light source, the bigger it is relative to your face, and the
softer the shadow edge. Same window at 8 feet away is a harsher, dimmer light
than at 2 feet. Distance is free and it does more than most lights do.

**4. One light source, one color.**
If any lamp or overhead is on while window light is coming in, the camera is
being handed 2 different color temperatures and it will pick a bad compromise.
Skin goes orange or green. Kill everything except the one source.

**5. Pick the right window.**
- North facing: soft, even, consistent all day. The best one in the house.
- East: good in the morning.
- West: good in the late afternoon and early evening.
- South: brightest, and harsh in the middle of the day. Tape a white bedsheet
  or a plain shower curtain over it and it becomes a large soft light.

**6. Bounce fill on the shadow side.**
Anything white, on a chair, just out of frame on the side away from the
window. White posterboard, a pillowcase, a folded white towel, a piece of
foam board from the closet. It throws light back into the shadow side of the
face and cuts the harshness in half. This is the trick that makes free light
look paid for.

**7. Get away from the wall.**
Sitting right against a wall puts your shadow on it and flattens everything.
Move the chair 3 or more feet off the wall. The background falls darker and
your face separates from it. Costs nothing, looks expensive.

**8. Clean the phone lens.**
Every phone lens has hand grease on it. With any light in frame, that grease
becomes haze and glow. Shirt, 2 seconds, noticeably sharper image.

## Filming at night, still free

Night in this house is the hard case. Order still holds: overhead off.

**A lamp in front of you, at or just above eye height, off to one side about
45 degrees.** Not behind you. Not overhead. If the lamp is too harsh, aim it
at a white wall or the ceiling IN FRONT of you and let the room bounce it
back. Bounced light is soft light.

**The laptop as a light.** Open a blank white document, brightness to max,
laptop propped just off camera at chest or chin height. It is a real soft
light source and it is already in the room. Second phone or tablet works the
same way, white screen, max brightness.

**Warm bulbs are fine at night** as long as they are the only source. Bad
color is consistency, not temperature. One warm light beats one warm light
plus one cool light every time.

## The phone setting that ruins more home video than bad light does

Auto exposure meters the whole frame. With a bright window in it, the phone
darkens everything to protect the window, and the face goes dark.

Fix, before you hit record every single time:
1. Tap and hold on your face until the exposure and focus lock.
2. Slide the exposure control down slightly, until the highlights outside the
   window stop blowing out but your face is still bright.
3. Lock it there and record.

Locking exposure also stops the shot from pulsing brighter and darker every
time you move.

## The structural fix, which is also free

Filming at night after a shift, in the worst light in the house, is fighting
the hardest version of this problem every single day.

Film in daylight, in one block, on a day off. Sit at the good window, get 5 to
10 takes in one sitting, and that block feeds a week of posts. Same outfit
across takes is not a problem, nobody is watching them back to back on 4
different platforms. This turns lighting from a daily obstacle into a weekly
decision that gets made once.

## When there's money for it

Skip the ring light. It gives flat, dead frontal light and puts a circle
catchlight in the eye that reads instantly as "content creator." The whole
point of this lane is person to person.

What to buy instead, in priority order:
1. **One LED panel with a diffuser and a stand.** Roughly 40 to 60 USD. Set it
   at 45 degrees to one side, slightly above eye level, angled down a little.
   That single light plus a white bounce card on the other side is a real
   2 point setup and it looks nothing like a house.
2. **A 5 in 1 reflector,** about 10 to 20 USD, if the posterboard version gets
   annoying. Same job, folds up.
3. **A small practical lamp in the background,** anything already in the
   house, to put a warm point of light behind you for depth.

Set any panel to about 4000 to 4500K if household bulbs will also be on. If
everything else is off, the temperature does not matter, just keep it the same
across takes.

One light, one bounce, one dark background. That is the entire setup.
