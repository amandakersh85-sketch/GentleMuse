# 19 Years Old, 10 of Them Mine

*What I've figured out about keeping a senior dog comfortable, written from our
living room and not a clinic.*

---

## Before you start

Her name is Princesa. Cesa. She's 19, she's a chihuahua, and I've had her for 10
of those years.

I need to say one thing before anything else, and I mean it plainly. I'm not a
vet. I'm not a trainer. I have no credentials of any kind. Everything in here is
what I noticed, what I changed, and what I asked someone qualified about. When
something in your house looks different, the answer is your vet, not this guide.
I'd rather be useful and honest than sound like an authority I'm not.

What I can tell you is what nobody told me.

Most of what I know I learned the slow way, by getting it wrong first and then
paying closer attention. There was a stretch around year 16 where I was reading
forums at 2 AM, trying to work out whether the thing she'd just done was normal
or the beginning of something. Nobody handed me anything. So I wrote the thing I
wanted back then.

It's short on purpose. None of it is complicated, and that's the point. You can
read it in 20 minutes and put down 2 of these things this week.

One more thing, because I think it's the whole frame. We're not chasing more
years. We're making the ones she has softer. Those are different jobs and they
need different decisions, and the second one is almost entirely within reach.

---

## 1. Walk the house at her height

This is the one I'd give you if you only took one.

Get on the floor. Actually do it, not a crouch. Lie down where she lies down and
look at the room from there. I did this the first time feeling ridiculous and
then spent the next hour changing things.

Standing up, you cannot see what she deals with. Here's what I found down there.

**The floor is a skating rink.** Old nails on hardwood or tile have almost no
grip, and a dog who slips once starts bracing before she walks. That bracing is
its own kind of tired. Runners, rubber-backed rugs, yoga mats end to end, cheap
and immediate. I laid a path from her spot to the water to the door and it
changed how willing she was to make the trip.

**Every turn costs her something.** Count the steps from where she sleeps to
where she drinks. Then count the turns. I took 2 turns out of that route by
moving a chair and it's the single laziest improvement I ever made.

**Transitions between rooms.** The little metal strip where tile meets carpet is
nothing to you. It's a curb to her. Same with the lip on a door frame.

**Light.** Her eyes aren't what they were and I didn't clock it for a long time,
because dogs compensate so well you don't notice until they stop. A low
nightlight near her route, not a bright one overhead, made her night trips less
hesitant.

**The corners at her head height.** Coffee table legs, the edge of a bed frame.
Padding on 2 of them was enough.

### The 3 feet rule

Here's the one I actually use now, and it came from getting it wrong.

She picked her spot in the kitchen years ago and she does not intend to
renegotiate it. When I rearranged that room, I moved the counter setup and
created a new path, and the new path went straight through where she sleeps. I
was carrying something hot the first time I realized it.

So now, before I move anything, I tape out her spot on the floor. Painter's
tape, 30 seconds. Then I plan around the tape.

**Measure her spot before you rearrange the room, not after.** Old dogs have the
house memorized. To her, a chair in a new place isn't a redecoration, it's an
obstacle that appeared overnight in a map she's been using for a decade.

And if you can help it, don't move things at all. Novelty is fun for you. It's
work for her.

## 2. Soft food, dentals, and what gumming actually means

Fair warning: this is the chapter where I most want to be clear that I'm out of
my depth on the medical side. Everything here went past a vet before it became
how we do things.

### The day I noticed she was gumming her dinner

She was eating, but wrong. Picking a piece up, working it at the front of her
mouth, putting it down. Doing that 3 or 4 times before actually swallowing
anything. It took me embarrassingly long to see it, because the bowl was
emptying, so I assumed dinner was fine.

The bowl emptying is not the same as dinner being fine. That's the sentence I
wish someone had handed me.

I called the vet, and what came out of that conversation was about her teeth. I'm
not going to pretend to explain the dental side, because I'd get it wrong and
you'd deserve better. What I'll say is this: if the mechanics of eating look
different, that's a vet conversation and not a food conversation, and I spent
weeks treating it as a food conversation first.

### What we changed after that, and it did help

**Warm water on her food, 10 minutes before.** Not hot. It softens things and it
brings the smell up, which matters more than I expected. Her nose is doing a lot
of the work of deciding whether dinner is worth the effort.

**I raised the bowl.** Eating off the floor means holding her head down and her
neck at an angle for the whole meal. A few inches up, propped on a book at first
because I was cheap about it, and she stayed at the bowl longer.

**Smaller, more often.** Same food, split across 3 sittings instead of 2. She ran
out of interest before she ran out of dinner, and spreading it out got more into
her without any fight about it.

**I stopped standing over her.** She eats better when I'm in the room but not
watching. I don't know why. It's just true here.

### The thing to actually keep an eye on

You can feel weight change before you can see it, especially on a small dog with
any coat at all. Hands on her ribs and her spine once a week, same time, so you're
comparing to something. I put a note in my phone.

Any change in how much she's eating, how fast, or what she weighs goes to your
vet. Not to a guide. Not to a forum at 2 AM, and I say that as someone who has
been on those forums at 2 AM.

## 3. The 3 second stare

Every person with an old dog does this and nobody talks about it.

She's asleep in a shape that should not be comfortable. Back legs straight out
behind her, chin flat on the floor, 1 ear folded under itself. And you stop
whatever you're doing and you stand there and you watch her ribs until you see
them move.

3 seconds. Then you go back to what you were doing.

I do it several times a day. I have never once been right to worry.

### Telling her worry from yours

Here's the honest thing: most of the staring is mine, not hers. It's my anxiety
about time, and she's just asleep. Learning to tell those apart is most of what
makes this age livable, because you cannot sustain 19 years of adrenaline.

What I actually check, and it takes about as long as the stare:

**Is she breathing evenly?** Not fast, not laboured, just even.

**Could she get up if she decided to?** Not is she getting up. Could she.

**Did she choose this position?** This is the real one. A weird position she
picked herself is a weird position. A position she ended up in, in a spot she
doesn't normally use, is a different thing and it's worth a closer look.

That last question sorts about 90 percent of it for me.

### Write it down, because you cannot see patterns from inside them

This is the tip I'd fight for.

Get a note in your phone. One line, whenever something seems off. Date, and what
you saw. Don't analyze it, don't decide what it means, just log it.

Day to day you'll notice nothing, because she changes slowly and you're with her
constantly and your eye adjusts. Across 2 weeks a pattern will be obvious. Across
2 months it'll be undeniable.

That note is also the single most useful thing you can hand a vet. "She seems a
bit off lately" is hard to work with. "These 6 dates, always in the evening, and
always after she's been on the hard floor" is something someone can actually use.

The only genuinely useful question in the moment is **is this new**. Not is this
bad. Just, is this new. And you can't answer that from memory. I've tried.

## 4. Sleep, and which changes matter

She sleeps most of the day now. That, by itself, has never been the thing.

### What turned out to be normal for us

**Sleeping more, and deeper.** She's harder to wake than she used to be and that
startled me at first. She's 19.

**Alarming positions.** See the previous chapter. She has a repertoire and it's
extensive and none of it has ever meant anything.

**Twitching, running feet, the small noises.** Same as when she was 3.

**Getting up slower.** This is the one that hurts to watch and it's just what's
happening. She hears the car before the door, and by the time I'm inside she's
already up, and getting up takes her longer than it used to. She still does it
every single time. I'm not going to pretend that doesn't get me.

### What I paid attention to instead

Not how much she sleeps. **Where**, and **whether the pattern changed.**

She has her spot. If she's sleeping somewhere she doesn't normally sleep, that's
information. If she's suddenly picking the cold tile when she's always chosen the
blanket, or the other way round, that's information.

Night waking and pacing was the one I took to the vet. Not because I knew what it
meant, I didn't, but because it was new and it kept happening and it was in my
notes with dates on it.

**Temperature is a bigger deal than I understood.** Small old dogs lose heat fast
and she got cold in rooms that felt fine to me. A blanket she can get under
rather than one laid over her, so she can decide. She uses it. That was a genuine
comfort improvement for almost no money.

### About accidents, briefly

She peed in my office and looked me dead in the eye about it. I got the towel.
She watched me do it from her bed.

Here's what I'll say, carefully. At this age it's usually not defiance, and it
isn't automatically the beginning of the end. In our case a lot of it came down
to practical things: how far the door was, how much traction was on the route,
and how long she could reasonably hold it. All 3 of those were things I could
adjust, and adjusting them helped.

But whether that's what's happening in your house is a question for your vet, and
it's worth asking early rather than deciding for yourself. I nearly didn't ask.

## 5. The 5 things I'd do differently in year 1

If I got a year 1 with her again.

**1. I'd have started the notes log years earlier.** Everything I know about her
patterns, I know from about the last 3 years. The 7 before that are a blur and I
have no baseline for any of it. It costs nothing and I'd have it forever.

**2. I'd have put traction down before she slipped, not after.** I waited for a
problem to appear. The rugs were always going to be the answer. She spent months
bracing on floors I could have fixed in an afternoon.

**3. I'd have stopped asking her to hurry.** Around year 14 I finally quit
rushing her out the door in the morning, and it turns out slow mornings were
never the problem, my schedule was. She gets up when she gets up now. The
mornings got better for both of us and I lost nothing I actually needed.

**4. I'd have asked the small questions instead of saving them up.** I used to
bank 5 or 6 minor things for the next appointment, which meant every one of them
went unanswered for months and 2 of them turned out to matter. Vets take
questions between visits. I didn't know that for an embarrassingly long time.

**5. I'd have taken more boring photos.** I have plenty of her looking cute. I
have almost none of her just lying on the floor in the room where I was working,
which is what she actually did for 10 years. The ordinary ones are the ones I go
back to. Take the boring photo.

## The last part

Different rooms, different jobs, different stretches I didn't talk about out
loud. The same small dog on the floor in the same 3 feet, chin down, waiting for
me to be done with whatever it was.

She was never impressed by any of it. That was sort of the point of her.

If you're somewhere in this with your own dog, I hope 1 thing in here was useful.
Don't do all of it. Pick the one that was easiest to picture in your house and do
that this week. The rest will still be here.
